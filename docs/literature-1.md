# Reroute FleetCentric shuttles as shortest covering paths

FleetCentric's incident rerouting is a known problem with a new twist: after a road block, finding the fastest bus path to campus such that every remaining stop lies within walking distance of a point where the bus actually stops is the **Shortest Covering Path Problem (SCPP)**, the fixed-endpoint cousin of the **Covering Tour Problem (CTP)**, with a median-type secondary objective (total walking) ([Current, Pirkul & Rolland 1994](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v28y1994i4p317-327.html); [Gendreau, Laporte & Semet 1997](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/oropre/v45y1997i4p568-576.html)). The problem is NP-hard in general, but a single shuttle with roughly 5 to 30 remaining stops is small, so the right build is an anytime ladder: an instant valid fallback, then "shortest path plus coverage check", then greedy insertion and removal, then an exact Held-Karp or CP-SAT solve under a hard time limit. The 0.1 CPU quota (10 ms of CPU per 100 ms, so roughly 10x wall-time inflation) and 512 MB cap rule out NetworkX/OSMnx graphs at runtime and make the stop-to-stop travel-time matrix, not the graph update, the real bottleneck; OSRM-MLD outside the container plus a scipy CSR fallback inside it handles both. Uber's H3 earns a place as a cheap filtering and indexing layer (incident zones, corridor pruning, stop pre-filtering, per-cell context) but must never be the walking or driving distance. Context-aware stops turn the single walking radius into a per-stop, per-situation radius and a must-serve flag, which Karachi evidence on harassment, darkness and flooding strongly motivates. The team can credibly claim a gap: no paper found combines mid-trip single-vehicle rerouting, a walking-coverage constraint with context-dependent radii, and a measured resource budget.

## Five decades of covering models already define the problem

The closest formal match is the SCPP: find a minimum-length path from a fixed origin s to a fixed destination t such that every demand node lies within distance r of some node on the path ([Current, Pirkul & Rolland 1994](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v28y1994i4p317-327.html)). Its bi-objective siblings are also native s-t path problems: the **Maximum Covering/Shortest Path Problem** trades path length against demand covered ([Current, ReVelle & Cohon 1985](https://api.crossref.org/works?filter=doi:10.1016/0377-2217(94)90149-X,doi:10.1016/0377-2217(85)90030-X,doi:10.1016/j.ejor.2010.03.045)), and the **Median Shortest Path Problem** trades path length against total demand-weighted access travel, explicitly framed as operator cost versus user cost and solved for Pareto points with a K-shortest-path method ([Current, ReVelle & Cohon 1987](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/ortrsc/v21y1987i3p188-197.html)). A branch-and-cut for the median-path problem adds lifted subtour inequalities and solves small and medium instances to optimality, but in up to two hours of 2005 CPU time, a warning that exact median-type models are not millisecond-class without care ([Avella et al. 2005](https://iaorifors.com/paper/49610)).

The CTP supplies the cleanest vocabulary. It partitions nodes into T (must visit), V (may visit) and W (must be covered within a distance of the tour) ([Gendreau, Laporte & Semet 1997](https://ideas.repec.org/a/inm/oropre/v45y1997i4p568-576.html)). For FleetCentric, **T = {bus position, campus}**, **V = candidate boarding points on the blocked road graph**, and **W = remaining stops**, with coverage c(w, v) = 1 when the walk from stop w to boarding point v is within the stop's radius. Any tour model converts to an s-t path by fixing a zero-cost arc from t back to s. Because the bus runs on a road graph rather than a complete graph, the Steiner TSP trick applies: compute shortest travel times between candidate nodes on the blocked graph (the metric closure) and solve on that small complete graph; compact Steiner TSP formulations solved instances above 200 nodes in CPLEX and are described as particularly suited to real road networks ([Letchford, Nasiri & Theis 2013](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/62788)).

One modelling decision matters more than any algorithm choice: **coverage should be measured from boarding points, not from any point on the route geometry**. The Close-Enough TSP lets a vehicle cover a target by passing within range anywhere along a street ([Golden et al. meter-reading slides](https://terpconnect.umd.edu/~bgolden/recent_presentation_pdfs_links/2006_nov_meter_reading.pdf)), which suits radio meter reading but not passengers, who need the bus to stop. DRT meeting-point work restricts candidates to OpenStreetMap locations "where a safe (de-)boarding is possible", not mid-junction ([Czioska et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.08488)), and covering from discrete points also lets the app tell riders "walk to point X". The user requirement "pass as close as possible to each stop" therefore becomes "stop at a boarding point within each stop's walking radius, minimising total walk as a secondary objective".

The objectives are lexicographic rather than a true trade-off: coverage is a hard constraint, travel time to campus is primary, and walking is secondary. Two standard ways to encode this are a weighted objective with a tiny walking coefficient, or the epsilon-constraint pattern used for the bi-objective CTP ([Jozefowiez, Semet & Talbi 2007](https://iaorifors.com/paper/59159); [Glize et al. 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v283y2020i3p812-824.html)): solve for minimum time, then minimise walking subject to time within (1 + δ) of that optimum. Offering a dispatcher two or three Pareto points (fastest, balanced, least walking) costs almost nothing at this instance size. On complexity, the notes' own reasoning (not read from a source) is that SCPP with r = 0 and every node as demand reduces to a shortest Hamiltonian path, so the general problem is NP-hard; the tractable special case, which the system should test first, is when the plain shortest s-t path on the blocked graph already covers every stop.

The dynamic-routing literature places this as an **event-driven re-plan of a partially executed route**, not a rolling-horizon problem with stochastic requests ([Pillac et al. 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v225y2013i1p1-11.html); [Psaraftis, Wen & Kontovas 2016](https://researchonline.ljmu.ac.uk/id/eprint/3846/)). In route-based MDP terms the state is (bus position, time, remaining stops, known closures) and the action is a new planned route ([Ulmer et al. 2020](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/On-modeling-stochastic-dynamic-vehicle-routing/9984380425102771)). With closures treated as deterministic once reported, a myopic re-solve from the current state is a defensible policy; anticipation only matters if the team models the chance of further closures, such as a spreading flood. The rerouting objective of Li et al. (operating cost, service cancellation cost, route disruption cost) maps directly onto travel time, stranded riders, and deviation from the published route ([Li, Mirchandani & Borenstein 2009](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v194y2009i3p711-727.html)).

## The 0.1 CPU budget turns the travel-time matrix into the bottleneck

Graph representation decides feasibility before any algorithm runs. On a synthetic 300,304-node, 1.02M-arc road-like graph benchmarked during this research (unpublished, unthrottled 2.8 GHz Xeon; the team must reproduce it on the Karachi extract), a **scipy CSR graph took about 38 MB with single-source Dijkstra in 0.05 s**, igraph about 118 MB and 0.26 s, a bare NetworkX DiGraph about 466 MB and 1.3 s, and an OSMnx-style NetworkX MultiDiGraph with typical attributes about **859 MB and 2.4 s**. At 0.1 CPU those times inflate roughly tenfold, so NetworkX cannot fit the memory cap or the time budget, while scipy leaves headroom on both. This matches published evidence: on a 3.07M-node graph, scipy-CSR-based scikit-network loaded in 1,222 MB while NetworkX overflowed memory ([Bonald et al. 2020](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.07660)), and on a 262k-node graph NetworkX shortest paths took 1.37 s against 0.05 s for igraph ([Lin benchmark](https://www.timlrx.com/blog/benchmark-of-popular-graph-network-packages-v2)). The practical rule is to load with OSMnx offline, export node ids, edge endpoints and travel times to numpy arrays, and build a CSR at container start; **a closure then becomes an O(1) write of infinity into the CSR weight array**.

For the distance oracle, the speed-up technique built for changing weights is Customizable Route Planning, which OSRM implements as MLD. In the original CRP paper on an 18M-node European graph, **a single edge change recomputes at most one cell per level in under a millisecond**, and a full re-customisation took a few seconds ([Delling et al. 2011](https://i11www.iti.kit.edu/extra/publications/dgpw-crp-11.pdf)); the survey reports a new cost function applied in roughly 370 ms on 12 cores ([Bast et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.05140)). OSRM's `osrm-customize` "can be run repeatedly without re-partitioning when speeds change", accepts a segment-speed CSV, and `osrm-datastore --only-metric` reloads weights without restarting the server ([OSRM docs v26.5.0](https://project-osrm.org/docs/v26.5.0/tools); [OSRM Traffic wiki](https://github.com/Project-OSRM/osrm-backend/wiki/Traffic)). Two facts remain unverified and are on the critical path: what a speed of 0 does in the segment-speed file (block or ignore), and how long `osrm-customize` takes on a Karachi extract on a small host. OSRM memory scales roughly linearly with input size from 123 GiB for the full planet ([OSRM memory wiki](https://github.com/Project-OSRM/osrm-backend/wiki/Disk-and-Memory-Requirements)), which puts a city extract in the low hundreds of MB, plausible but tight alongside a solver, so keeping OSRM outside the 512 MB container is the safer design.

Contraction Hierarchies give the fastest queries but need partial or full re-contraction after weight changes ([Bast et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.05140)). **ALT stays exact when arc costs only increase**, and a closure is an increase to infinity, so ALT needs no recomputation at all ([Bast et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1504.05140); [Goldberg & Harrelson 2005](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/computing-the-shortest-path-a-search-meets-graph-theory)). D* Lite and LPA* reuse earlier search effort and showed several-fold fewer expansions than repeated A* on grids ([Koenig & Likhachev 2002](https://cse-robotics.engr.tamu.edu/dshell/cs625/aaai02b.pdf)), but no city road-network evaluation was found and their advantage shrinks when the change sits near the start, which is exactly a bus about to hit the block. With exact Dijkstra already at about 0.5 s wall time in scipy, in-house CH, CCH or D* Lite is not worth the engineering for the FYP. Mobile CH gives a useful existence proof: exact routing ran on a 400 MHz handheld in 56 to 73 ms with about 16 bytes per node, with preprocessing done on a PC ([Batz et al. 2008 slides](https://ae.iti.kit.edu/english/1554.php)), the same split as OSRM outside and a light solver inside.

The less obvious consequence is where the time goes. A single shortest path is cheap; **the metric closure is not**. With 20 remaining stops and 3 candidate boarding points each, the solver needs travel times among about 62 nodes. Computed in-container with scipy, that is about 62 Dijkstras at 0.05 s CPU each, roughly 3 s of CPU or **about 30 s of wall time at 0.1 CPU**, which blows a few-second budget. Three remedies follow from the notes. First, one OSRM `/table` call after re-customisation computes the whole matrix outside the quota. Second, only matrix rows and columns whose cached shortest paths crossed the closed edge change, so most of the pre-incident matrix survives. Third, an H3 corridor (below) shrinks the in-container graph so each fallback Dijkstra is far cheaper. Walking cover sets need no runtime work at all: compute them offline per stop on the pedestrian graph up to the 800 m cap and filter by the context radius at runtime.

With the matrix in hand, the solver should be an anytime ladder, following the anytime framing of trading deliberation time for result quality ([Zilberstein 1996](https://www.doi.org/10.1609/AIMAG.V17I3.1232)) and the "fast feasible, then tighten" behaviour of ARA* ([Likhachev, Gordon & Thrun 2003](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~maxim/files/ara_nips03_abs.html)). Stop-skipping research supports exact search at this size: Sun and Hickman solved their real-time skipping model by exhaustive search because instances were small ([Sun & Hickman 2005](https://trid.trb.org/view.aspx?id=756819)).

| Rung | Method | Cost | Guarantee |
|---|---|---|---|
| R0 | Published plan with the blocked leg patched by a detour (one shortest-path call) | Milliseconds | Valid immediately, no skipping |
| R1 | Shortest s-t path on blocked graph; test coverage at every boarding point along it | One Dijkstra plus set lookups | Optimal whenever it covers all stops |
| R2 | Greedy repair: insert the boarding point with least detour per newly covered stop, recompute coverage along the realised path, then try removing each waypoint; or an order-preserving DP over the published sequence (O(n^2)) | Well under 1 s with a precomputed matrix | Heuristic |
| R3 | Held-Karp DP over needed waypoints (practical up to about 15), or CP-SAT `AddCircuit` with self-loops for skipped nodes, coverage constraints per stop, warm-started from R2, hard time limit | Seconds | Optimal on the metric closure, or best found by deadline |
| Optional | LNS destroy-and-repair with simulated-annealing acceptance on a fixed budget | Fixed budget | Anytime improvement |

The greedy and DP rungs are the notes' design proposals rather than published methods; LNS follows GLNS for the generalized TSP ([Smith & Imeson 2017](https://eceweb.uwaterloo.ca/~sl2smith/GLNS/)) and ALNS ([Ropke & Pisinger 2006](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/references/10.1287/trsc.1050.0135)). OR-Tools routing with Guided Local Search is the usual off-the-shelf alternative, but its default time limit is effectively infinite, so an explicit `time_limit` is mandatory ([OR-Tools routing options](https://developers.google.com/optimization/routing/routing_options)), and whether that limit counts wall or CPU time under a CPU quota is unverified. More importantly, "visit at least one of the boarding points in C(w)" does not map cleanly onto OR-Tools disjunctions when cover sets overlap, which makes CP-SAT or a small MIP the cleaner exact route (an inference the team should confirm). Deep reinforcement learning for the Covering Salesman Problem reports more than 20x speedups over heuristics ([Li et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.05875v1)), but at FleetCentric's scale it is related work, not a method to adopt. One more correctness rule: coverage computed on the metric closure is conservative, because the realised road path passes extra nodes that can cover stops for free, so always recompute coverage along the realised node sequence.

## H3 works as a filter and index, never as the distance

H3 is a 64-bit-integer, aperture-7 hexagonal grid with 16 resolutions; **res 9 averages 201 m edges and 0.105 km2**, **res 10 averages 76 m and 0.015 km2**, and children are only approximately contained in parents ([H3 resolution table](https://h3geo.org/docs/core-library/restable/); [Uber H3 blog 2018](https://www.uber.com/blog/h3/)). Hexagons have a single neighbour class with near-equal centre spacing, so a k-ring is a near-circular buffer; S2 squares have two neighbour classes but exact parent containment, and geohash cell size varies with latitude ([H3 vs S2](https://h3geo.org/docs/comparisons/s2); [H3 vs geohash](https://h3geo.org/docs/comparisons/geohash)). A local benchmark in Karachi (unpublished, h3-py 4.5.0, unthrottled) measured res 10 neighbour spacing of 126 to 134 m and a 78 m circumradius, about 1 µs per `latlng_to_cell`, and 6 to 70 µs per `grid_disk` for k = 1 to 5. **Even at 10x slowdown, H3 operations are negligible against a seconds-scale budget**; shortest-path work dominates.

The literature pattern is consistent and worth copying: H3 or hexagons decide where things are and what gets aggregated, while a street-network router decides how far apart they are. Brazil's Access to Opportunities project aggregates everything on a res-9 H3 grid but computes travel times between hexagons with r5r over GTFS and streets ([aopdata manual](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/packages/aopdata/aopdata.pdf); [IPEA AOP](https://ipea.gov.br/acessooportunidades/en/project/1_aop_project)); a Santiago study uses res-9 H3 zones with R5py routing ([Marin-Flores et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.21743)); DRT and transit-equity studies on hexagon tessellations compute walking with OSRM ([Leonardi et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2501.01898); [Badeanlou et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2210.00128)). Routing directly on a hex mesh appears only in open-water maritime work ([Tsatcha, Saux & Claramunt 2014](https://oskar-bordeaux.fr/handle/20.500.12278/78042?show=full)), and `grid_path_cells` ignores roads and is not stable across library versions ([H3 traversal API](https://h3geo.org/docs/api/traversal)). Karachi's one-ways, flyovers and nullahs make hex distance a poor proxy for either driving or walking.

Within that rule, H3 has five concrete jobs in FleetCentric. **Incident zones** become cell sets: a 2 km by 2 km block converts to 306 res-10 cells (78 after compaction) in under 0.5 ms in the local benchmark, and removing graph edges whose midpoint cell is in the set costs one hash lookup per edge; multiple incidents are set unions and expiry is set difference. Polygon-to-cells uses centroid containment by default, so a thin blocked road can be missed at coarse resolution; buffer the road geometry first or use the experimental overlap mode ([Crunchy h3-pg docs](https://access.crunchydata.com/documentation/h3-pg/latest)). **Corridor pruning** builds a res-8 corridor from the bus cell to the campus cell buffered by k = 3 or 4 rings (331 cells for Saddar to FAST NUCES in the local benchmark), keeps only road nodes whose precomputed cell falls inside, and widens automatically if no path exists, since a corridor can exclude the true detour around a long blockage. Parent truncation misassigned 6.4% of Karachi points from res 10 to res 7 in the local benchmark, so index points directly at each resolution used and pad coarse sets with a 1-ring. **Stop pre-filtering** uses an inverted index from cell to stops. The arithmetic needs care: for a 400 m radius at res 10, a path cell within k = 1 ring of a stop guarantees a straight-line distance under about 290 m (1 x 134 + 2 x 78), and no path cell within k = 4 rings guarantees more than about 474 m (5 x 126 - 2 x 78). Because network walking distance is always at least the straight-line distance, **H3 can safely exclude stops but cannot confirm coverage**; anything not excluded goes to the exact walk-graph check. **Travel-time caching** stores typical road times between res-8 or res-9 cell centroids by time-of-day bucket, useful for ranking candidate stop subsets or as an A* heuristic, but not as a final ETA given intra-cell variance. **Context aggregation** puts boardings, incident history and speeds per res-9 cell, matching the walking-scale accessibility literature, and per-cell OSM context embeddings such as Hex2vec and highway2vec exist if the team later wants learned features ([Woźniak & Szymański 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00970); [Leśniara & Szymański 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.13865)).

Storage should be integers: in the local benchmark a 1,493-cell buffered path took 11.9 KB as a numpy uint64 array versus about 179 KB as a Python set of ints, and a dense float32 cell-pair matrix was about 10x smaller than a dict keyed by cell pairs. On the database side, **Supabase's extension catalogue includes PostGIS and pgRouting but not h3-pg** ([Supabase extensions](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/extensions)), so the workable pattern is to compute H3 in Python, store `h3_r9` and `h3_r10` as indexed `bigint` columns, pre-filter with `= ANY($1::bigint[])`, and run exact checks with PostGIS `ST_DWithin` or pgRouting. Pentagons sit at icosahedron vertices, mostly in oceans ([Uber H3 blog 2018](https://www.uber.com/blog/h3/)), so they will not arise in Karachi, but the code should still catch H3 grid-navigation errors.

## Context decides who may be skipped and how far they walk

Semi-flexible transit already distinguishes stops by service requirement. Demand Adaptive Systems keep **compulsory stops** served within time windows and **optional stops** served only when slack allows, with a policy (DAS2) that tolerates relocating drop-offs "nearby" but not pickups ([Errico et al. 2011/2013](https://www.cirrelt.ca/DocumentsTravail/CIRRELT-2011-64.pdf)). MAST caps how much slack early insertions may consume so later demand is not starved ([Quadrifoglio, Dessouky & Palmer 2007](https://lquadrifoglio.engr.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/221/2021/03/JoS_MAST.pdf)). School bus routing with stop selection assigns each student to any stop within a maximum walking distance D while routing the bus ([Schittekat et al. 2013](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v229y2013i2p518-528.html); [Calvete et al. 2020](https://zaguan.unizar.es/record/95502/files/texto_completo.pdf)), and its benchmark instances use a single global D. FleetCentric's reroute is that problem restricted to one bus, with blocked edges removed and **D replaced by a context-dependent radius r_i(c)**, a one-line change to the Calvete allocation constraint. A context-aware stop, in Dey's sense of context as information characterising the situation of an entity ([Dey 2001, search listing](https://www.academia.edu/992448/Understanding_and_Using_Context)), is a stop whose must-serve flag, radius and prize are functions of a context vector c (time and light, weather or flood, incident location, registrations, onboard count, deadline slack).

The defaults have reasonable support. DRT simulation uses **about 400 m as acceptable and 800 m as the maximum** walk ([Czioska et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1709.08488)), and Montreal data put the **85th percentile walk to a bus at about 524 m**, with the authors arguing for tailored rather than fixed buffers ([El-Geneidy et al. 2014](https://www.worldtransitresearch.info/research/5119)). The evidence for context multipliers is mixed, and the report should say so. Sydney data found transit mode dominates walking distance while age, gender and time of day were not significant ([Daniels & Mulley 2013](https://jtlu.org/index.php/jtlu/article/view/308)), and a review calls evidence on weather and walking "incomplete and fragmented" ([Böcker, Dijst & Prillwitz 2013](https://liser.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/impact-of-everyday-weather-on-individual-daily-travel-behaviours-)). That null result concerns walks people chose in Sydney, not a mandated night walk in Karachi. Local evidence points the other way: among Karachi female domestic workers surveyed in 2021, **89% feared sexual harassment, 46% left home after dark, and many walked up to 30 minutes to reach a stop** on roads with scarce sidewalks ([Anwar & Shakeel 2023](https://mhrc.lums.edu.pk/disconnected-mobility-infrastructures-female-domestic-workers-the-perils-of-bus-transport-in-karachi)); women's mobility in Pakistan is roughly half of men's and heavily walking-dependent ([Adeel, Yeh & Zhang 2017](https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/transp/v44y2017i6d10.1007_s11116-016-9712-8.html)); and Karachi has no dedicated storm drainage, with Shahrah-e-Faisal, Gulshan-e-Iqbal, Saddar and underpasses routinely submerged ([IIPS 2022](https://iips.com.pk/urban-flooding-the-case-of-karachi/)). No source quantifies a multiplier such as "rain cuts acceptable walk by X%", so any numbers FleetCentric uses are assumptions to calibrate.

The notes propose a concrete formulation. The radius is r_i(c) = min(R_max, r_base x m_light x m_weather x m_safety x m_type), with r_base = 400 m and R_max = 800 m, and **r_i = 0 (must-serve) when a rider needs accessibility or the context flags walking from that stop as unsafe**. Context also enters the pedestrian graph: flooded walking links are removed and risky links penalised, following safe-navigation work that scores edges by crime density and offers both total-risk and max-risk variants ([Galbrun, Pelechrinis & Terzi 2014](https://members.loria.fr/EGalbrun/resources/GPT14_safe.pdf)), so a stop is covered only if its walk to a boarding point is within radius and its riskiest segment is under a threshold. Directly served stops earn a prize p_i(c) proportional to registrations and walking hardship, which turns the model into a prize-collecting covering path ([Balas 1989](https://api.crossref.org/works?filter=doi:10.1287/trsc.1030.0079,doi:10.1002/net.3230190602,doi:10.1287/ijoc.1110.0480)). This deliberately reverses an efficiency result: on Manhattan taxi data, about one minute of average walking cut rejected requests by more than 80%, and riders in high-demand areas were the ones asked to walk ([Fielbaum, Bai & Alonso-Mora 2021](https://research.tudelft.nl/en/publications/on-demand-ridesharing-with-optimized-pick-up-and-drop-off-walking/)). FleetCentric should make the choice explicit: big stops get high prizes, but the radius stays per rider.

Two further points follow from the notes' reasoning rather than a source. Direction matters: Sun and Hickman's variant keeps drop-offs inside a skipped segment ([Sun & Hickman 2005](https://trid.trb.org/view.aspx?id=756819)), and for a campus-bound morning run every skipped stop affects waiting riders who must walk before the bus arrives, so **a rider's walk time to the new boarding point should not exceed the bus's ETA there**, a MAST-style time window. For the evening run the asymmetry flips, because the drop-off is where a student walks home, often after dark. Weights can start hand-set and later be fitted, either by a distance-decay logistic model of whether notified riders actually boarded, or by coordinate descent against dispatcher-approved reroutes as in personalised routing ([Delling et al. 2015](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=332444)) and context-to-preference learning ([Guo et al. 2020](https://pure.ecnu.edu.cn/en/publications/context-aware-preference-based-vehicle-routing/)).

## Experiments should close real Karachi roads and plot quality against time

The motivation sentence is strong: in a World Bank study of 2,564 cities, a 1-in-100-year flood exposed about **14.7% of urban roads but caused 44.8% of simulated trips to fail**, and surviving trips added about 1.5 km and 3 minutes ([He et al. 2022](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/developmenttalk/mobility-and-resilience-global-assessment-flood-impacts-urban-road-networks)). A few closed links on key corridors disable a large share of routes, so rerouting is a core feature, not an edge case.

Scenario generation should avoid the trap of closing random roads no shuttle uses, which makes every method look perfect. The notes recommend four families restricted to edges on or near shuttle routes: uniform random single-edge closures for average behaviour; **betweenness-ranked closures**, with both static and recalculated rankings for multi-edge closures since recalculated attacks are often more harmful ([Holme et al. 2002](https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0202410)); **importance-ranked closures** where each edge's importance is the increase in time to campus when it closes ([Jenelius, Petersen & Mattsson 2006](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transa/v40y2006i7p537-560.html)); and **historical Karachi scenarios**. Two are citable: the 24 February 2024 protest closure of Shahrah-e-Faisal between Karsaz and FTC with traffic diverted to Korangi Road ([Aaj English TV](https://english.aaj.tv/news/30352283)), and the University Road ponding model between Hasan Square and NIPA, where a 100-year one-hour storm produced a maximum delay of about 35 to 39 minutes ([Hussain, Ahmed & Ali 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40534-018-0155-0)). The second argues for supporting partial speed reductions, not only binary blocks. Closures should also be injected at different points along the trip (before the first stop, mid-route, just before the blocked edge), because disruption location changes which skip policy wins ([Sun & Hickman 2005](https://trid.trb.org/view.aspx?id=756819)). No open, machine-readable Karachi incident feed was found, and Waze's closure feed is inbound to Waze rather than a consumable source ([Waze closure docs](https://developers.google.com/waze/data-feed/road-closure-information)), so a dispatcher or driver "report closure" button plus a hand-built historical library from news archives is the realistic path.

Baselines should separate the routing question from the context question. For routing: R0 (patched published route, no skipping), plain shortest path to campus ignoring stops (a lower bound on time and an upper bound on stranding), the greedy and DP rungs, and an **oracle**, the exact R3 solve on a fresh full matrix with no time limit. For context: (A) no skipping, visiting every remaining stop by the shortest detour; (B) a fixed 400 m radius; (C) context-aware radii, prizes and must-serve flags. Context "helps" if (C) cuts unsafe or over-limit walks relative to (B) without a large loss in arrival time, and dominates (A) on time.

The metric set mirrors published practice. Disruption papers report the share of passengers unserved and itinerary cost against benchmark strategies, for example 7.3% unserved and 27.0% and 43.3% cost reductions in a Beijing road-disruption study ([Zheng et al. 2024](https://www.worldtransitresearch.info/research/10583)); stop-skipping work reports passenger waiting time ([Sun & Hickman 2005](https://trid.trb.org/view.aspx?id=756819)); and alternative-route work reports success rate and query time ([Abraham et al. 2010](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2010/01/alternativeSea2010.pdf)). For FleetCentric that becomes **delay versus oracle** (mean, p95, share within one minute, re-evaluated on true post-closure times); **rider impact** (riders skipped, added walking metres per affected rider, riders stranded beyond their radius, night-time walks on low-safety segments); **plan stability** (stops reordered relative to the published sequence, echoing route-disruption cost); **fairness** (max/min or Gini of walking burden across stops over many scenarios, proposed here rather than taken from a source); and **compute** (wall-clock decision latency and peak RSS measured inside `--cpus=0.1 --memory=512m`, OSRM calls made, time to first feasible plan). The headline figure should be an **anytime performance profile**: gap to oracle against time budget (0.1 s, 1 s, 5 s, 30 s), with each rung as a point, in the spirit of Zilberstein's quality-versus-time framing ([Zilberstein 1996](https://www.doi.org/10.1609/AIMAG.V17I3.1232)). A separate ablation should measure H3 corridor pruning's speedup and how often it excludes the true optimal detour, since no study evaluating this was found.

## The defensible gap sits at the intersection of three literatures

Each ingredient exists separately. Covering-path models (SCPP, CTP and relatives) are mature but evaluated as static network-design problems; recent work focuses on exact column generation for multi-vehicle and bi-objective CTP ([Glize et al. 2020](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/ejores/v283y2020i3p812-824.html)) and prize-collecting CTP ([IJISE 2023, title only](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ids/ijisen/v45y2023i1p101-134.html)). Transit disruption research handles holding, stop-skipping, deadheading, short-turning and bus bridging ([Ibarra-Rojas et al. 2015](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transb/v77y2015icp38-75.html); [Gkiotsalitis & Cats 2021](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transe/v145y2021ics136655452030819x.html); [Zhu, Jin & Wang 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/transe/v188y2024ics136655452400228x.html)), but these keep fixed route geometry or redesign whole networks with column generation and ALNS stacks far too heavy for 0.1 CPU. School bus stop selection uses a single global walking limit. Context-aware routing personalises path choice for individual travellers, not skip decisions for a shared vehicle.

The team can therefore claim, with the caveat that the searches were not exhaustive and several databases were rate-limited: **(1) real-time, mid-trip rerouting of a single fixed-route shuttle as a shortest covering path on a blocked road network**, which no paper found addresses; **(2) a covering model with context-dependent, per-stop walking radii and must-serve flags, grounded in Karachi safety and flooding evidence**, for which no prior formulation was found; and **(3) an empirical evaluation under an explicit 0.1 CPU / 512 MB budget**, reporting anytime quality-versus-time curves and engine-call counts, metrics that do not appear in the transit-rerouting papers reviewed. H3-based corridor pruning for road routing is a smaller, secondary contribution, since no evaluation of it was found either.

## Conclusion

The research reframes FleetCentric's hardest feature from "invent a rerouting algorithm" to "pick the right 40-year-old model and engineer it to fit a tiny box". That shift matters for the FYP defence: the formulation borrows its legitimacy from SCPP and CTP, so the contribution lies in what those papers never had, a moving bus, a live closure, riders whose acceptable walk depends on darkness, flooding and safety, and a hard compute budget. The single most important engineering finding is counterintuitive: updating the road graph after a closure is nearly free, while recomputing the travel-time matrix among candidate boarding points is what threatens the deadline, so the design should be organised around avoiding that work (OSRM `/table` outside the quota, reuse of unaffected rows, offline walking cover sets, corridor pruning).

The biggest open risks are empirical rather than theoretical, and each is cheap to retire early: confirm OSRM's behaviour for zero-speed segments and its customise time on the Karachi extract, re-run the graph-memory benchmark on the real OSM graph under an actual CPU quota, measure Karachi's walking detour factor so H3 straight-line bands translate into network-walk thresholds, and treat every context multiplier as a calibratable assumption rather than a fact. If those hold, a single Python service with scipy, OR-Tools CP-SAT and h3-py can deliver provably good reroutes within seconds, and the evaluation design above gives the team a publishable comparison rather than a demo.
